Modeling and Neural-Network Control of an Asymmetric Soft Pneumatic Actuator

Created2020-09-01
|Post View:

About this project

This is one of my undergraduate research projects at Zhejiang University, done in Prof. Tiefeng Li’s group at the Soft Matter Research Center. We built a soft pneumatic “muscle” with three asymmetric air chambers, modeled its mechanics, and controlled it with a neural-network adaptive controller that uses camera and IMU feedback.

We wrote it up as Mechanical Modeling and Neural Network Based Control of Asymmetric Soft Pneumatic Actuators (Zhiwei Xu, Haijun Wang, Mingqi Zhang, Zheng Chen, Tuck-Whye Wong and Tiefeng Li) and submitted it to the journal Soft Robotics. It came back with a minor revision, but after we revised it, the paper was rejected. It was never published, so this note is where the work lives now: a summary of what we did, with the figures from the manuscript.

The idea

Rigid robot arms driven by electric motors are fast and precise, but they aren’t good at handling soft or fragile things. Soft pneumatic actuators are much gentler, but they are hard to model and hard to sense: they deform nonlinearly, have many degrees of freedom, and embedded strain sensors tend to drift, wear out, or heat the silicone.

So the plan was:

  1. Design an actuator that can both bend and twist (two degrees of freedom) using three chambers.
  2. Sense its shape from the outside, by fusing a depth camera with an IMU on the tip, so there’s nothing embedded in the silicone.
  3. Instead of tuning a hand-derived dynamic model, let a neural network learn the model’s parameter matrices online, inside a controller that is still provably stable.

Design and fabrication

The actuator was inspired by PneuNets and Toshiba’s Flexible Microactuator. It has one large main chamber and two smaller auxiliary chambers arranged asymmetrically, 120° (1.047 rad) apart. Inflating the main chamber bends the actuator; inflating the auxiliary chambers twists the bending direction.

Parameter Symbol Value
Height of the main chamber $h_1$ 15 mm
Height of the auxiliary chamber $h_2$ 4.27 mm
Distance from main chamber bottom to axis $t_1$ 7 mm
Distance from auxiliary chamber bottom to axis $t_2$ 6.4 mm
Width of main chamber units $w_1$ 9 mm
Width of auxiliary chamber units $w_2$ 11 mm
Gap between main / auxiliary units $b_1, b_2$ 1 mm
Number of main / auxiliary units $N_1, N_2$ 12 / 10
Length $L$ 120 mm
Mass $m$ 59.89 g

The molds were 3D-printed in water-soluble PVA, so they could simply be dissolved away instead of pried out. The steps were:

  1. Pour Dragon Skin 20 silicone into the molds.
  2. Cure for about an hour at 65 °C.
  3. Soak in a 90 °C water bath for an hour to dissolve the PVA.
  4. Glue the parts together and attach the air tube.

The finished actuator can bend up to 1.35 rad and twist by ±0.25 rad. When it’s used as a finger, that’s enough to move the contact point (by bending) and fine-tune the force direction (by twisting) so objects don’t slip.

Sensing: depth camera + IMU

An IMU is attached to the free end of the actuator, and a depth camera watches it from the side. The camera has blind spots, and the IMU drifts over time; fused together with an Extended Kalman Filter, they cover each other’s weaknesses. The filter gives the tip position, and differentiating it gives the speed.

Static model

The static model describes how chamber pressures map to bending and twisting. From torque balance, the stress at the base of chamber $i$ is

$$\tau_i = \frac{p_i\,(h_i + t_i)^2}{t_i^2}$$

The chamber stresses combine into an equivalent stress, which is split into a bending component and a twisting component. Treating the actuator as a pure-bending beam shaped like an arc ($\tau = E\epsilon = E d / R$) gives the general form of the bending angle $\varphi$ and rotation angle $\theta$ as functions of the pressures.

To pin down the coefficients, I ran ABAQUS simulations and collected 12,244 samples of the actuator’s end positions under different pressures, then fit the formula by least squares. With $p_1$ the main-chamber pressure and $p_2$ the auxiliary-chamber pressure:

$$\varphi = \sqrt{(75p_1)^2 + (7.5p_2)^2 - 1125\,p_1 p_2}, \qquad \theta = \arctan\frac{\sqrt{3}\,p_2}{20p_1 - p_2}$$

Dynamic model

Everything runs on ROS. A neural-network controller node subscribes to the fused position and speed and to the pressure sensors, and sends commands to electric proportional valves and exhaust valves.

I used the bending angle and the rotation angle as generalized coordinates, $q_1 = \varphi$ and $q_2 = \theta$, and modeled the bent actuator as an arc of radius $R = L/q_1$. That gives the kinetic energy of bending and twisting, and the potential energy from gravity plus the elastic energy of the silicone (Yeoh hyperelastic model; the chambers barely change width, which simplifies the strain invariants). Plugging them into the Lagrange equations gives the familiar manipulator form:

$$H(q)\,\ddot{q} + C(q,\dot{q})\,\dot{q} + G(q) = \tau$$

Here $H$ is the inertia matrix, $C$ the Coriolis/centrifugal terms, and $G$ gravity plus elasticity. Assuming the valve flow is proportional to the command voltage and treating the chamber volume as roughly constant, pressure becomes the integral of flow, and the whole plant from voltages $U$ to angles $Q$ can be written as

$$\frac{Q(s)}{U(s)} = \frac{1}{s\left(H(s)\,s^2 + C(s)\,s + G(s)\right)}$$

This model was useful for understanding the system, but tuning a controller around it by hand would have been slow and fragile, which is where the neural network comes in.

Neural-network adaptive controller

Following adaptive and robust neural control of rigid manipulators, three small networks (three layers, five neurons each) approximate the model matrices, $H \approx H_{SNN}$, $C \approx C_{SNN}$ and $G \approx G_{SNN}$. Their weights $W_H$, $W_C$ and $W_G$ are updated online.

With the tracking error $e = q_d - q$, a filtered error $r = \dot{e} + \Lambda e$, and reference signals $\dot{q}_r = \dot{q}_d + \Lambda e$ and $\ddot{q}_r = \ddot{q}_d + \Lambda\dot{e}$, the control law is

$$u = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\hat{H}_{SNN}\ddot{q} + \hat{C}_{SNN}\dot{q} + \hat{G}_{SNN}\right) + K_p\dot{r} + K_i r + K_r\,\mathrm{sgn}(r)$$

The first term is the model-based part estimated by the networks, the next two are PI-style feedback on the filtered error, and the last is a robust term that absorbs the networks’ approximation error. Each weight vector follows an adaptive law of the form $\dot{\hat{W}}_k = \Gamma_k \xi_k r_k$, where $\xi_k$ is the hidden-layer output. Using the skew symmetry of $\dot{H} - 2C$, a Lyapunov function built from $r$, its integral, and the weight errors has $\dot{V} \le 0$, so the closed loop is stable.

Experiments

I tested the actuator on step, sine, square-wave and triangle-wave targets at two frequencies (0.1 and 0.2 rad/s). In every case the controller goes through a short learning phase with larger errors, then locks on within about one period, and the remaining error stays small.

Faster targets are harder. At 0.2 rad/s the actuator still follows the sine wave, but it lags the target a little and the error roughly doubles. The twisting direction can also be tracked, though it’s noisier because the rotation range is small (about 0.2 rad).

Square waves are the hardest case: each jump produces an error spike (about 0.8 rad), which then decays quickly as the actuator catches up. Triangle waves are tracked closely at 0.1 rad/s, with some lag at 0.2 rad/s.

The frequency limit comes from the whole loop: sensor sampling, signal transmission and computation. The further the target frequency is below that limit, the smaller the error.

Takeaways

  • External sensing (depth camera + IMU, fused with an EKF) was good enough to close the loop on a soft actuator without putting any sensors inside the silicone.
  • A neural network can learn the dynamic model’s parameter matrices quickly, typically within the first period, inside a controller whose stability can still be proven with a Lyapunov argument.
  • The main limitation is speed: higher-frequency targets mean larger errors. Faster sensors and valves, and a lighter-weight algorithm, would be the next step, as would combining the actuator with tactile sensing for grasping.
