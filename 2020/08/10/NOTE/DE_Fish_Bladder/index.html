An Artificial Fish Swim Bladder Made of Dielectric Elastomer

Created2020-08-10
|Post View:

About this project

This was my project during a summer 2020 internship on underwater robotics at The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen, in Prof. Zhenglong Sun‘s Robotics and Artificial Intelligence Lab. A real fish changes the volume of its swim bladder to control its buoyancy, so the goal was an artificial “swim bladder” for a robotic fish: a small chamber whose volume can be changed electrically, with no pump and no motor. The actuator I chose was a dielectric elastomer (DE), a soft film that changes shape when you put a voltage across it.

This note is based on my end-of-project report, and covers the background research, the design, how I made it, the results, and what I learned along the way.

How dielectric elastomers work

A DE actuator is basically a soft capacitor. A thin, stretchy insulating film (the dielectric elastomer) is coated on both sides with compliant electrodes. Here the electrodes were carbon grease, which stays conductive as the film stretches. When a high voltage is applied, the opposite charges on the two electrodes attract each other. This electrostatic pressure, called the Maxwell stress, squeezes the film: it gets thinner and, because the material is incompressible, its area grows.

Background research

I started by surveying the DE actuator designs in the literature. The common configurations are:

  • Butterfly and diamond frames, where a stretched film pulls a hinged frame open or closed.
  • Extender (stacked) actuators, where many thin layers are stacked so their thickness changes add up.
  • Push-pull actuators, two films working against each other.
  • Unimorph and bimorph benders, where one or two active layers on a passive layer make it curl.
  • Diaphragm actuators, a film clamped over a hole that bulges out when energized.
  • Spring-roll (rolled) actuators, a film wrapped around a compressed spring that extends along its axis.

A swim bladder needs a change in volume, so the diaphragm and spring-roll ideas were the most useful starting points.

First try: a spring-roll linear actuator

To learn the fabrication process, I first built a spring-roll actuator. A compressed spring serves as the pre-load, and two layers of DE film are wrapped around it. The outer layer is only there to seal in the carbon grease, which is runny and would otherwise smear onto everything. The steps were:

  1. Calculate the pre-stretch ratio and make a frame to match.
  2. Stretch the elastomer over the frame, leaving a 1 cm margin.
  3. Brush carbon grease on both sides of the active layer as the electrodes, and run out a strip of conductive foil from each.
  4. Laminate the two layers together, leaving a channel for trapped air to escape.
  5. Roll the film around the compressed spring and fix both ends.
  6. Apply a voltage: the film softens and the spring pushes the actuator longer.

Final design: the bladder

The final bladder is a rigid cylinder with a DE membrane clamped over each end and a compressed spring inside. The spring pushes the two membranes outward, keeping them pre-stretched. To make the membranes stronger and the actuation larger, each one is a stack of five DE films with four carbon-grease electrodes between them. That forms two capacitors connected in parallel and driven from one side.

When voltage is applied, the membranes soften and the spring pushes them further out, so the volume inside grows. When the voltage is removed, the membranes pull back and the bladder returns to its rest volume.

How it was made

  1. Make the frames, one for each membrane.
  2. Stretch the elastomer film over the frame, leaving a 1 cm margin.
  3. Brush a ring of carbon grease on the film as an electrode, and run out a strip of conductive foil.
  4. Repeat four times, so four electrodes end up between five layers of film.
  5. Clamp the stack between two fixing plates with screws, so the film can’t shrink back.
  6. Mount the membranes on both ends of the cylinder with the spring compressed between them. The spring keeps the membranes stretched and closes off the chamber.

Results

I tested the bladder with a weight sitting on the membrane as a pre-load, and switched the high voltage on and off.

The change is much easier to see in motion:

Video (14 s, no sound): the bladder on the lab bench, held in a red frame with a metal weight on top as the pre-load and wired to a high-voltage supply, showing the membrane deforming under voltage.

What I learned

  • Parallel capacitors are a trade-off. Putting two DE capacitors in parallel increases the capacitance and the force, but it also lowers the breakdown voltage.
  • Thinner is stronger. The closer the positive and negative electrodes are, the larger the deformation for the same voltage.
  • Bubbles are fatal. Any air bubble trapped between the layers will cause electrical breakdown, which is why the air-escape channels and careful lamination mattered so much.
