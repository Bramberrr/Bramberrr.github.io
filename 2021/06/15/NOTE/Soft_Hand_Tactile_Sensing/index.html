A Tactile Nervous System for a Bionic Soft Robotic Hand

Created2021-06-15
|Post View:

About this project

This was my undergraduate thesis in Automation (Control) at Zhejiang University, completed in June 2021. My advisor was Prof. Tiefeng Li, in whose group I had done research since 2018, and Prof. Ruifen Hu also helped a great deal with the project.

Soft robotic hands are great at grabbing delicate or irregular objects, but they are hard to give a sense of touch: ordinary rigid sensors can’t bend and stretch with a silicone finger. In this project I designed and built stretchable strain sensors (how much a finger bends) and pressure sensors (how hard it presses on something), embedded them in a three-finger bionic soft hand, and built a ROS system that shows what the hand “feels” in real time while it grasps.

Why soft sensors are hard

Researchers have tried many ways to sense a soft robot’s shape and contact: commercial flex sensors, carbon nanotubes, liquid strain sensors, hydrogels, optical fibers, and cameras. Three problems kept coming up:

  • Mismatched materials. A sensor that is stiffer than the finger changes how the finger moves, and the two age at different rates.
  • Low accuracy. Contact sensors drift, and resistive flex sensors can be unstable because silicone traps heat. Cameras are accurate but slow.
  • No system-level view. Most work studies one sensor on its own, but when grasping, bending and pressure affect each other and need to be read together.

My plan was to build two kinds of sensors (resistive and capacitive) for each of the two measurements (strain and pressure), compare all four, and integrate the best combination into the hand.

Materials and methods

The sensors were made from silicone rubber (Dragon Skin 20) and PDMS, with conductors made of liquid metal (a gallium–indium alloy that is liquid at room temperature, which I made by heating and magnetically stirring the two metals), nano-copper powder, or carbon grease. Molds were printed on a Formlabs SLA printer.

Before building anything, I simulated each design in ABAQUS (hyperelastic material models, explicit dynamics) to check that it would respond usefully inside a bending finger. Each sensor was then tested for three things:

  • Sensitivity: the relative change in resistance or capacitance per newton of force, $\frac{\Delta R / R_0}{F}$ or $\frac{\Delta C / C_0}{F}$.
  • Measurement range: the forces between which the reading actually changes.
  • Response time: how long the reading takes to rise from 10% to 90% of its final value after the force changes.

Resistive strain sensor

A conductor’s resistance is $R = \rho L / (w h)$. When the finger bends, a conductive channel embedded in it gets longer and thinner, so its resistance goes up.

The ABAQUS simulation confirmed this: as the inflated finger bends, the channel’s cross-section shrinks, its length grows, and its resistance rises.

To make the sensor, I printed a mold with the channel pattern, coated it with about 400 µm of silicone, and filled the channels with a 7:3 mix of conductor and PDMS, which keeps most of the conductivity but cures into a stretchable solid. Then I baked it and attached wires.

Conductor Sensitivity Range Response time
Liquid metal + PDMS 0.04 N⁻¹ 0.36–9.2 N 20 ms
Nano-copper + PDMS 0.05 N⁻¹ 0.41–9.6 N 26 ms
Carbon grease + PDMS 0.01 N⁻¹ 0.11–9.8 N 50 ms

Grasping produces strain forces below 5 N, and all three sensors responded smoothly, changing for every 0.05 N or so and returning to the same value when released.

Resistive pressure sensor

The pressure sensor has three layers: a top electrode layer with a spiral of broken circular loops, a ring-shaped silicone spacer, and a copper conductor layer at the bottom. Pressing on it pushes the top layer down onto the copper, which shorts out some of the loops. The harder you press, the more loops are shorted and the lower the resistance.

Conductor Sensitivity Range
Liquid metal + PDMS 0.3 N⁻¹ 2.2–10.0 N
Nano-copper + PDMS 0.5 N⁻¹ 1.6–10.0 N
Carbon grease + PDMS 0.2 N⁻¹ 1.8–10.0 N

These sensors respond to changes of about 0.5 N, but they show nothing at all below about 2 N: the top layer has to bend far enough to touch the copper first. That dead zone is built into the design, so they aren’t suitable for gentle grasps.

Getting here took a few failed attempts. Pure liquid metal leaks out of the channels, and powders break up and lose contact. That’s why every conductor was mixed with PDMS and cured into a stretchable solid.

Capacitive strain sensor

A capacitor’s capacitance is $C = \varepsilon S / d$. In a soft capacitor, stretching makes the dielectric layer thinner and the electrodes larger, so the capacitance goes up.

The electrodes are liquid metal mixed with PDMS, set into 10 mm × 80 mm recesses in silicone on stretchy fabric. The dielectric is porous PDMS: mixing PDMS with water and heating it boils the water off, leaving pores. With 40% water, its relative permittivity is about 2.18, which gave good sensitivity. The two halves are each coated with 100 µm of this dielectric and then bonded together.

It was more sensitive than the resistive version, about 0.01 N⁻¹ at small forces, worked from 0 to 30 N, and responded quickly with little hysteresis. But it had one serious problem: it also reacts strongly to pressure, even more than to stretching. On a finger that is squeezing something, the two signals would get mixed up.

Capacitive pressure sensor

The capacitive pressure sensor uses the same principle with small disk electrodes: pressing on it brings the plates closer together and increases the capacitance. Each finger carries one sensor strip with four sensing points, so the hand has 12 pressure points in total.

It was about 0.01 N⁻¹ sensitive over 0.5–10 N, followed the force closely, and responded in under 20 ms. Unlike the capacitive strain sensor, it barely reacts to stretching.

Choosing the combination

After comparing all four, I chose:

  • Resistive strain sensors (liquid metal) to measure bending: low resistance, high sensitivity, fast, stable, and not confused by pressure.
  • Capacitive pressure sensors to measure contact force: sensitive, no dead zone, fast, and not confused by stretching.

The tactile nervous system in ROS

An Arduino Mega reads the 3 strain sensors and 12 pressure sensors, converts the readings to numbers, and publishes them as a ROS message (sensor_msg) over USB, using rosserial. A PC running ROS on Ubuntu 20.04 subscribes to that topic and turns the data into markers in rviz. I compiled and uploaded the Arduino firmware from the ROS workspace with catkin_make, so both sides were built together.

In rviz, each green curve is one finger, bent to match the strain reading, and the red ticks along it are the four pressure points, with longer ticks for more force.

Limitations

  • Handmade sensors. Filling the channels and wiring was done by hand and by eye, so every sensor behaved a little differently and needed its own calibration. That used up memory on the Arduino, and sensors couldn’t be swapped between positions.
  • Built for one hand. The sensors were made together with these specific fingers and can’t easily be reused elsewhere.
  • Coarse pressure map. A 3 × 4 grid of pressure points is enough for simple grasps, but not for something like a cactus.
