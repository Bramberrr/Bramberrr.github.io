The "3D Printer Car": Our SUPCON Cup Robot

Created2019-06-15
|Post View:

About this project

This is the robot my team built for the 14th SUPCON Cup Robot Competition at Zhejiang University, in spring 2019. It was my second time in the competition. I led a team with two teammates, and we entered under the name “Bamboo Rats: 3 Yuan Each, 3 for 10”, a meme that was popular at the time.

The robot drives around a field of grid lines, stops at the intersections, and picks up objects with a gripper. This note is adapted from our technical report.

The idea: a 3D printer on wheels

From earlier experience, I expected that a gripper mounted on a robot arm would wobble, especially when the motor current isn’t steady. A 3D printer, on the other hand, positions its nozzle very precisely along X, Y and Z rails. So instead of an arm, we built a small 3D-printer-style gantry on top of a car. The gripper moves along straight axes, which keeps it steady while lining up and after it has picked something up.

Mechanical design

Chassis. Four DC gear motors with wheels and couplings drive the robot. The chassis is two layers of acrylic connected by copper standoffs. The space between the layers holds the battery, the low-level controller, the motor drivers, a breadboard and the switches.

Upper body. The upper body is a wooden box that works like a 3D printer, with four motions:

  • Up and down (Z): a stepper motor turns a lead screw, and the nut raises or lowers the whole upper body. Guide rods and aluminum angle keep it aligned.
  • Forward and back (X): a stepper motor drives a timing belt over pulleys, moving a slider along guide rods to extend or retract the gripper.
  • Left and right (Y): the same belt-and-slider setup on the other axis, for fine sideways adjustment.
  • Gripper: a digital servo opens and closes an iron claw. The jaws are padded with heat-resistant sponge for grip.

Motor selection

We used JGA25-370 gear motors: 12 V, 10 kg·cm of torque, with 6.5 cm wheels. By our calculation, four of them can move at least 10 kg, far more than the robot weighs.

Sensors

The robot has 8 infrared line sensors: 4 at the front and 4 in the middle. The front row follows the line and counts the grid lines it crosses. The middle row tells the robot exactly when it is centered on an intersection, so it can stop there and turn accurately.

Each sensor board is a 4-channel infrared module. Each channel has its own IR emitter and receiver, and an LM339 comparator with hysteresis turns the analog signal into a clean on/off output without flickering at the threshold. The modules run on 4–6 V and draw 30–40 mA with all four channels on.

Electronics

  • Low-level controller: ATmega128. An 8-bit AVR microcontroller (up to 16 MIPS at 16 MHz, 128 KB flash, 4 KB EEPROM, 4 KB SRAM). We chose it mainly because it has enough I/O pins for all the motors and sensors.
  • High-level controller: Raspberry Pi with a camera, for image recognition. It sends commands to the ATmega128.

What made it different

  1. A gantry instead of an arm. The 3D-printer-style structure moves the gripper along straight axes, so the robot stays steady and doesn’t shake.
  2. Two rows of line sensors. Separate rows for following and for stopping made the turns at intersections much more precise.

What was hard

  1. Line following sometimes lost the line, and counting grid lines wasn’t always reliable: the robot could overshoot and miss a line.
  2. Communication delay between the Raspberry Pi and the ATmega128.
  3. Power: with four DC motors, three steppers and a servo, the battery struggled to supply enough current.
